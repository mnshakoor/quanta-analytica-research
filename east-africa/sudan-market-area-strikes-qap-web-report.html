01 // BLUF

Bottom Line Up Front

1
[ASSESS] Sudan's market-area strike pattern is recurrent and geographically clustered, not adequately explained by isolated tactical error.

Open-source evidence identifies repeated strikes impacting markets and civilian economic nodes across Darfur and the Kordofan belt. OHCHR reported at least 28 market attacks causing civilian casualties during January-April 2026, and the priority incidents are clustered in RSF-controlled or contested towns. [S1] [S4]

2
[ASSESS] H4 is the best-fit hypothesis: military targeting near markets plus unlawful attacks on civilian objects.

The evidence supports a mixed pattern. Some incidents are tied to alleged military or logistics activity near markets, while others show direct or foreseeable civilian-market harm. H2, recurrent reckless or permissive targeting, remains the strongest underlying judgment. [S3] [S5] [S7]

3
[CAVEAT] SAF is the dominant alleged actor in the market-strike subset, but attribution remains incident-specific.

Six of the seven priority incidents are attributed in the source set to SAF-linked air or drone activity. That judgment is not uniform legal attribution. Belligerent denials, anonymous military accounts, missing munition remnants, and incomplete platform attribution keep confidence below High. [S2] [S3] [S15]

4
[FACT] The humanitarian effect is severe because markets are survival infrastructure.

Markets in Sudan's conflict economy function as food, fuel, liquidity, transport, remittance, and informal aid nodes. Repeated strikes can reduce trader movement, fuel availability, and food access while increasing displacement and health-system burden. [S5] [S6] [S16]

5
[ASSESS] Legal-risk exposure is high, but the public record does not prove criminal responsibility for all incidents.

Civilian markets are protected civilian objects unless they become military objectives. Even where combatants or logistics are present nearby, distinction, proportionality, and precautions remain binding. The current evidence supports investigation into possible direct, indiscriminate, or disproportionate attacks. [S11] [S12]

05 // EVIDENCE

Evidence Summary

What We Know
  • OHCHR reported drone strikes caused at least 880 civilian deaths from January-April 2026.
  • Market attacks with civilian casualties were repeatedly reported in the same period.
  • Priority incidents include Ghubaysh, Saraf Omra, Adikong, Al-Muglad, Abu Zabad, Wad Banda, Al Safiya, and Tora.
  • Tora is the strongest verified case due to imagery and satellite validation reported through Reuters and CIR.
What We Assess
  • Markets are being treated as logistics-adjacent battlespace when fuel, vehicles, roads, or armed actors are alleged nearby.
  • Repeated peak-market harm indicates a permissive or reckless targeting environment.
  • H4 best explains the data because both military-adjacent targeting and unlawful civilian-object harm appear in the same pattern.
  • External supply chains likely contribute to scale and reach, but incident-level platform attribution remains incomplete.
What We Do Not Know
  • Exact market footprints for all priority sites.
  • Original imagery chain-of-custody for multiple cases.
  • Named or de-identified hospital intake records for several incidents.
  • Munition-remnant photographs for the seven priority market cases.
  • Operator chain, launch platform, and target-selection record.
WSI Source Independence Note: Load-bearing judgments rely on multiple collection pathways: UN/human rights reporting, humanitarian medical intake, reputable wire reporting, technical geolocation, regional reporting, and legal reference sources. Non-independent repetition, belligerent claims, and social media posts are not counted as separate corroboration streams.
Key Drivers: FACT expanded drone use; INFERENCE target-category drift from markets to logistics nodes; INFERENCE civilian-harm normalization; FACT contested attribution and denial narratives.
Binding Constraints: conflict-zone access limits, incomplete forensic evidence, contested information environment, rapid media syndication, and high harm potential from publishing sensitive details.

07 // BEHAVIORAL

Applied Behavioral Tradecraft

Behavioral analysis is used here only where it improves conflict risk modeling. It does not infer individual psychology. It models observable group incentives, framing, and decision patterns.

01

Target-category drift

Markets are increasingly framed as logistics nodes when fuel, roads, vehicles, or armed actors are alleged nearby.

Implication: civilian-object protection is degraded through operational framing.
02

Civilian-harm normalization

Repeated strikes despite known crowd density indicate tolerance for foreseeable harm, even when intent is contested.

Implication: each unaccountable event lowers the threshold for the next.
03

Narrative denial loop

Belligerents use denial, hostile-force framing, and counter-accusation to manage accountability pressure.

Implication: incident documentation must separate event, casualties, weapon, actor, and intent.
08 // IMPLICATIONS

Strategic Implications, Risk and Recommendations

A. Strategic Implications

For humanitarian actors: Market days, fuel queues, hospital-adjacent markets, and cross-border trading points should be treated as elevated-risk nodes. Distribution models that create predictable civilian concentrations near contested logistics should be stress-tested.

For legal and human rights actors: The evidence supports investigation into possible direct, indiscriminate, or disproportionate attacks. Legal work must keep event occurrence, civilian harm, weapon type, operator, target intent, and proportionality separate.

For diplomatic and sanctions actors: Arms-transfer controls, drone components, ground-control systems, optical payloads, guided munitions, and transit logistics are priority monitoring targets.

09 // CONFIDENCE

Confidence and Uncertainties

Overall Confidence: Moderate-High

Confidence is elevated by recurrence across multiple independently reported incidents, the presence of high-quality UN and humanitarian sources, and technical verification in the Tora case. Confidence is constrained by incomplete market-footprint geolocation, missing munition-remnant photographs for the priority set, and contested attribution in several incidents.

Flip-Risk Uncertainties

Platform-to-incident linkage: confirmed munition or drone remnants tied to two or more market strikes would increase confidence in H3.
Co-location evidence: verified evidence of systematic RSF military co-location in markets would pull the assessment toward H2 and away from H3 elements.
Coordinate correction: verified geolocation for Ghubaysh and Al Safiya could either strengthen or weaken clustering and intent analysis.

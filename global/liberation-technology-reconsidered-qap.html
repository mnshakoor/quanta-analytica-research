01 // BLUF
Bottom Line Up Front
01
The central analytical question is not whether digital technology can empower populations. It is which forms of digital empowerment can produce durable institutional change when political authorities retain control over underlying infrastructure.

FactDiamond argues that the internet, mobile phones and social media can widen expression, support mobilization and expand freedom, while also noting that the outcome depends on political organization and strategy as well as on technology [1][3]. FactArezki argues that social media has not freed closed regimes because autocrats control the off switch, and that digital payments are a more durable pathway because they enlarge the economy that rulers themselves depend on [2][4].

02
Political mobilization and political liberalization are different outcomes, and control over infrastructure is the intervening variable that separates them.

AssessBoth authors treat infrastructure control as consequential, Diamond by acknowledging that authoritarian states can use the same tools to suppress protest, Arezki by making the off switch central to his argument [2][6]. The two sources therefore agree on the constraint and disagree on which technology survives it.

03
Economic empowerment is a plausible but incompletely demonstrated pathway to political change. Neither model is protected against institutional or commercial capture.

CaveatArezki ties political effects to a conditional mechanism: a new digital middle class whose demand for the rule of law drives change when it is a challenger rather than an incumbent [2]. AssessThat condition is the weak point. If the beneficiaries are co-opted, or if payment rails are repurposed for surveillance, the pathway closes. The most defensible integrated explanation places accountable institutions, not any single technology, at the center of durable freedom.

03 // SNAPSHOT
Situation Snapshot
Who
Larry Diamond, founding co-editor of the Journal of Democracy, and Rabah Arezki, director of research at CNRS and former chief economist at the African Development Bank [3][4].
What
Two competing theories of which digital technology can expand freedom: communications platforms (Diamond) versus digital-finance infrastructure (Arezki).
Where
Closed and hybrid regimes in general. Neither source is reduced to a single country in the material reviewed.
When
Diamond: July 2010, vol. 21, no. 3. Arezki: October 2026, vol. 37, no. 4, pp. 40-50 [3][4].
Why
Social media proved easier for regimes to censor, surveil and switch off than early optimism assumed, which reopens the question of where durable leverage lies [2][5].
How
Diamond: expression, exposure and mobilization. Arezki: incentive-compatible payments, erosion of authoritarian rents, and a new digital middle class that demands rule of law [1][2].
04 // KEY JUDGMENTS
Key Judgments
High confidenceKJ-1KIQ-1

Political mobilization and political liberalization are different outcomes. Mobilization is an event-level result; liberalization requires durable changes in rules, enforcement and accountability. Diamond himself places the final outcome in political organization and strategy rather than in the technology [3].

BASIS: [1][3] definitional distinction | SINGLE-SOURCE CAVEAT: not applicable, both sources consistent
Moderate confidenceKJ-2KIQ-2

Control over infrastructure is a decisive intervening variable. A technology that a regime can disable or repurpose at low cost will not, on its own, generate durable institutional change. Both authors reach this constraint from different directions [2][6]. Confidence is moderate because the two authors publish in the same venue and the claim has not been tested here against independent data.

Arezki reports grounding in Findex data, mobile money and tax revolts, and conditions the political effect on whether the new middle class is a challenger or an incumbent [2]. The full text, and therefore the strength of that evidence, was not reviewed.

05 // EVIDENCE
Evidence Summary
Know
  • Diamond's essay appeared in July 2010 (vol. 21, no. 3) and describes the internet and mobile phones as tools that enable expression, mobilization and wider freedom [3].
  • Diamond's essay concludes that the contest between democrats and autocrats depends on political organization and strategy as well as technology [3].
  • Arezki's essay appeared in October 2026 (vol. 37, no. 4, pp. 40-50) [4].
  • Arezki's stated claim is that autocrats control the off switch for social media, whereas payments are incentive-compatible for rulers [2].
Assess
  • Infrastructure control separates mobilization from liberalization (KJ-2).
  • Payment-led empowerment is plausible but conditional on the political position of the new middle class (KJ-3).
  • Capture risk is symmetrical across both technology layers (KJ-4).
  • Institutions are the convergent explanatory layer (KJ-5).
Unknown
  • How [2] defines and measures durable institutional change.
  • The temporal ordering of payment adoption and institutional demand in the cases [2] cites.
  • How prevalent state access to payment-transaction data is in closed regimes.
  • Whether the "challenger versus incumbent" condition can be identified in advance.

Key Drivers

  • Driver 1 Regime cost of disabling the technology. Low for social platforms, high for payments, according to [2].
  • Driver 2 Immediate economic utility to citizens, which produces adoption without a political motive [2].
  • Driver 3 Organizational capacity of civil society and the new middle class to convert capability into demands [3].

Binding Constraints

  • STRUCTURAL. State ownership or licensing control of networks and payment rails.
  • POLITICAL. Whether ruling coalitions treat the emerging digital middle class as a threat or as a client.
  • RESOURCE. Access to the full text of [2] and to comparative data for this product.
  • TEMPORAL. Arezki's essay is days to weeks old as of this assessment; no critical response or replication exists in the material reviewed.
WSI / source independence note. Seven sources are registered, but they reduce to two independent analytic voices: Diamond and Arezki. Sources [3] and [6] derive from Diamond's own work or editorship, and sources [4] and [5] derive from Arezki. Convergence between the two authors is therefore informative, but it is convergence of two scholars, not corroboration by independent data.

6.5 Scenarios (Conceptual)

Best case

Capacity meets accountability

Digital-finance expansion coincides with independent revenue and consumer-protection institutions. The new middle class operates as a challenger and its demands are institutionalized. This is the pathway Arezki describes [2].

Probability: not assigned; evidence base insufficient for calibration.
Base case

Economic gains, political closure

Payments expand and welfare improves, but the digital middle class is co-opted or absorbed as an incumbent. Rents shift rather than erode. Liberalization does not follow.

Probability: not assigned; analyst inference.
Worst case

Rails become instruments of control

Payment infrastructure is used for surveillance and conditionality, and communications platforms are already constrained [5]. Both technology layers are captured.

Probability: not assigned; analyst inference.
07 // BEHAVIORAL
Applied Behavioral Tradecraft

Justification: the pathway from technology to institutional change runs through the incentives of rulers, citizens and intermediaries. Behavioral analysis is therefore used to rank which incentives are most likely to determine outcomes. Rankings below are analyst inference unless a source is cited.

01

Ruler survival and revenue calculus

Rulers weigh the political risk of a technology against its fiscal benefit. Arezki's argument depends on payments tilting that calculus toward tolerance [2].

INFERENCE: tolerance lasts only while the fiscal benefit exceeds the perceived political cost.
02

Citizen adoption driven by utility, not politics

Adoption of payments follows immediate economic benefit, which lowers the barrier to participation compared with explicitly political platforms [2].

INFERENCE: low political salience at adoption may delay political demand.
03

Intermediary incentives

Platform firms and payment providers respond to licensing and regulatory pressure from the state that controls market access.

INFERENCE: no source reviewed tests this; relevant to KJ-4 and PIG-3.
04

Middle-class position as challenger or incumbent

Arezki conditions the political effect on whether the new digital middle class remains a challenger [2].

INFERENCE: co-optation through contracts, licenses or protection converts challengers into incumbents.

Narrative Vulnerabilities

Two narratives require care. First, technological determinism: the claim that a tool, by its nature, liberates. Both sources qualify this, Diamond by naming organization and strategy, Arezki by naming conditions on the middle class [2][3]. Second, a replacement narrative: the claim that payments supersede communications. The evidence reviewed supports a narrower statement, that payments may be harder for rulers to suppress at low cost. The wider literature remains divided on whether the internet erodes or expands democracy [7].

08 // IMPLICATIONS
Strategic Implications, Risk and Recommendations

Implications for Local Actors (Governments and Civil Society)

AssessRegimes that restrict communications platforms may still promote payments for fiscal reasons [2]. Civil-society actors who rely only on communications platforms remain exposed to the off switch. Strategies that combine economic inclusion with organizational capacity are more resilient than platform-only strategies, consistent with Diamond's emphasis on organization [3].

Implications for International Actors (Donors, Development Banks, Democracy-Support Bodies)

AssessArezki's framing implies that outside actors may support democratic evolution indirectly through financial inclusion [2]. The assessment here is that such support should be paired with institutional safeguards, such as data-protection rules and independent oversight, because the pathway is conditional and capture risk is symmetrical (KJ-4).

Implications for Regional and Commercial Actors

AssessPayment providers and platform firms operate inside state licensing regimes. Their design choices on data retention and access determine whether the infrastructure can be repurposed for control. This is analyst inference and a priority collection gap.
